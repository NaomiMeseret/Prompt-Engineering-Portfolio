# Module 1 Reflections — The Savannah Adventure
### By Naomi Meseret

---

## What Changed In My Thinking

Before Module 1, I thought using AI meant typing a question and reading the answer — like a smarter Google search. I did not think about structure, context, or responsibility.

Three things fundamentally shifted:

---

### Shift 1: I Am Responsible For The Output

The most uncomfortable truth in this module is that when AI gives a bad answer, the fault is usually mine — not the AI's.

Before: "The AI gave me a useless response."
After: "My prompt gave AI nothing to work with."

This shift matters because it puts me in control. A bad answer is not a dead end — it is feedback telling me to refine my question.

---

### Shift 2: Vagueness Is Dangerous, Not Just Unhelpful

I used to think a vague prompt just produced a vague answer — annoying but harmless.

Module 1 showed me that in high-stakes contexts like healthcare, finance, or education, a vague prompt can produce genuinely harmful outputs. An AI that gives a pregnant woman in rural Uganda generic Western nutrition advice is not just unhelpful — it could cause real damage.

Precision is not perfectionism. It is responsibility.

---

### Shift 3: AI Has No Local Knowledge Unless You Give It

AI was mostly trained on Western internet data. It does not know about matooke harvest cycles, M-Pesa payment constraints, boda boda travel times, or SACCO financing structures — unless I tell it.

This means the burden of localization is on me. I am the bridge between AI's global training and my local reality. That is not a limitation — it is power. It means someone who deeply understands their local context will always get better results from AI than someone who does not.

---

## The Hardest Moment In This Module

The hardest moment was when my mentor refused to write the AfyaTech assignment for me — twice.

I kept deflecting with multiple choice questions instead of answering in my own words. My mentor called it out directly: I was hiding behind answer options because they felt safer than being wrong.

That discomfort was real. And it was exactly what the module described — "The discomfort is just the sound of your mind debugging."

When I finally wrote my own introduction, it was better than anything I could have copied. That was the most important lesson of Module 1 — not a framework, but a mindset.

---

## One Sentence I Want To Remember

"A bad prompt shouts into the wind. A good prompt listens to the rustle in the grass and strikes with economy."

---

## What I Will Do Differently

Every time I open an AI tool from now on, before typing anything, I will ask myself three questions:

1. Have I given it an Actor, Input, and Mission? (AIM)
2. Have I grounded it in my local reality with real data? (MAP)
3. Before I trust the output — have I verified it? (OCEAN)

Those three questions are my new default.
